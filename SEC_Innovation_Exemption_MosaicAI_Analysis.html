2The 60-Second Version

TL;DR

The SEC just gave a green light — for five years, on a trial basis — to a new way of trading stocks on the blockchain. Instead of requiring every trading venue to become a registered stock exchange, the SEC created a temporary carve-out ("innovation exemption") for platforms called Tokenized Securities Venues (TSVs). These venues can let people trade tokenized versions of already-public, exchange-listed stocks (think a blockchain token that represents one share of Apple or Tesla) using automated pricing robots called AMMs (automated market makers) instead of a traditional buyer/seller order book.

Important nuance: this exemption only covers "NMS stocks" — i.e., stocks already listed on a U.S. public exchange like the NYSE or Nasdaq. It does not cover tokenized private assets such as private real estate, private equity, or private credit — which is exactly what platforms like MosaicAI issue. So the direct legal effect on MosaicAI today is small — but the signal it sends is large, especially because it landed just 48 hours after Congress's own crypto market-structure bill, the CLARITY Act, collapsed in the Senate.

3What Did the SEC Actually Do?

Normally in the U.S., if you want to run a venue where people buy and sell stocks, you have two choices: become a registered national securities exchange (like the NYSE) or a registered Alternative Trading System / broker-dealer. Both come with heavy compliance obligations built for a world of human market makers and centralized order books.

The SEC's order says: for a specific, limited, closely-watched pilot, a new kind of venue — a Tokenized Securities Venue (TSV) — does not have to register as an "exchange," and the people supplying the trading capital ("liquidity providers") don't have to register as a "dealer," as long as everyone follows a specific rulebook (see Section 5).

SEC Chair Paul Atkins framed it as four core conditions: TSVs must be qualified, U.S.-domiciled entities that comply with OFAC sanctions rules; trading is permissioned, restricted to eligible participants only; synthetic tokenized stocks are banned — a token must be issued by the real issuer (or its agent), or tokenized by an unaffiliated third party, and must carry the same dividend, voting, and other rights as the underlying share; and the underlying issuer has the right to object to and block its stock from being traded on a given TSV. Ordinary anti-fraud and anti-manipulation rules under federal securities law continue to apply in full — this exemption removes a registration requirement, not investor-protection law.

In short: the "exchange" is replaced by code. Instead of a centralized order book matching buyers and sellers, an automated market maker (AMM) — a smart contract holding a pool of two assets — sets the price mathematically and settles instantly on a public blockchain.

4How an AMM Liquidity Pool Actually Works

Think of an AMM pool as a big glass jar that always holds two things — say, tokenized Apple stock ("tAAPL") and digital dollars (USDC) — in a ratio that implies a price. The most common formula is the constant product formula:

Formulax × y = k (constant)
xtokenized shares in the pool
yUSDC (dollars) in the pool

Sample calculation

Starting pool1,000 tAAPL & 250,000 USDC
Implied price before trade250,000 ÷ 1,000 = $250.00
Constant, k1,000 × 250,000 = 250,000,000
Investor buys10 tAAPL
New tAAPL in pool1,000 − 10 = 990
New USDC in pool250,000,000 ÷ 990 = 252,525.25
USDC investor pays252,525.25 − 250,000 = $2,525.25
Average price paid per share$252.53 (vs. $250.00 quoted) → 1.01% slippage

That 1% gap is called slippage — the cost of trading against a mathematical formula instead of a deep order book. It shrinks as the pool gets bigger (more liquidity) and grows for larger trades or thinner pools. This is precisely why the SEC's conditions (next section) cap trading volume and require public, auditable contracts: a shallow or manipulated pool can move price sharply.

5The Guardrails the SEC Attached

The exemption is deliberately narrow and conditional. Key conditions from the order:

ConditionPlain-English meaning
U.S. entity + OFAC complianceA TSV must be a qualified U.S.-domiciled operator and screen for sanctioned parties — no offshore, anonymous venues.
Permissioned access onlyTrading is restricted to eligible, verified participants — not an open, anyone-can-trade public pool.
No synthetic stockA token must be a real claim on the actual share (issued by the issuer/agent, or tokenized by a vetted third party) — a look-alike "synthetic" derivative that just tracks the price is banned.
Equal rights for token holdersA tokenized Apple share must carry the same dividend, voting, and other rights as a real Apple share.
Issuer objection & block rightThe underlying company can object to — and outright block — its stock from being tokenized and traded on a given TSV. Stronger than a courtesy notice: it's a veto.
Symbol & volume limits (two tiers)Tier 1 (S&P 500 / Russell 1000 names): up to 75 symbols, capped at 0.25% of average daily volume. Tier 2 (other NMS stocks): up to 250 symbols, capped at 2.5% of ADV.
Public, auditable smart contracts on permissionless chainsThe trading code must be visible and checkable by anyone, and can't run on a private, closed blockchain the operator controls.
Synchronized trading haltsIf the NYSE/Nasdaq halts trading in the real stock (e.g., news pending, circuit breaker), the tokenized version must halt too.
Anti-fraud & anti-manipulation rules still applyExisting federal securities law protections aren't waived — only the exchange/dealer registration requirement is.
Public disclosure & recordkeepingTSVs must publish trade data (prices, sizes, timestamps, pool addresses, end-of-day pool sizes, daily volumes) and disclose their own trading activity on the venue.
Temporary: 5-year pilot → possible permanent ruleThe exemption expires ~September 2031; the SEC will collect public comments and then consider writing a permanent rule, rather than making this permanent today.
Dealer exemption for liquidity providersFirms that put their own proprietary capital into the AMM pool are exempted from registering as broker-dealers — but must trade solely for their own account and may not hold or custody customer assets.
Scope limit — read twice

This order is about NMS stocks only — tokenized wrappers of shares already trading on a registered U.S. exchange. It is not a general blockchain-trading law, and it explicitly does not touch private securities, Reg D placements, real estate, private credit, or venture equity — the categories MosaicAI works in.

Industry reaction: AMC's Adam Aron

AMC Entertainment Chairman & CEO Adam Aron publicly praised the decision, describing it as a five-year pilot built on mandatory investor protection: no synthetic tokenized stocks, full voting and dividend rights attached to every token, and issuers retaining the right to object to trading of their own securities on a TSV. He went further and called on Robinhood to apply those same three standards (investor protection, no synthetics, issuer objection rights) to its tokenized-stock offerings outside the U.S. — a reminder that the "rulebook" in this section is quickly becoming the industry's de facto global benchmark, not just a U.S. compliance checkbox.

6Context: This Landed Right After the CLARITY Act Died

Two days before this order, on September 15, 2026, the U.S. Senate failed a procedural ("cloture") vote on the CLARITY Act (formally the Crypto Market Clarity Act, H.R. 3633) — the most significant attempt yet to write a comprehensive federal rulebook for digital assets, including which regulator (SEC vs. CFTC) oversees which tokens. The vote failed 49–50 — affirmative votes came in below even 50, well short of the 60 needed to open debate — with three Republicans (Collins, Hawley, Moran) voting no alongside unified Democratic opposition tied partly to concerns about the President's personal crypto holdings. Coverage of the vote and reaction is widely discussed, including in commentary videos like "The Clarity Act is DEAD" and an interview with the Solana Policy Institute's Kristin Smith on the backlash.

What was actually in the bill Democrats rejected

Per WuBlockchain's reporting, Senate Republicans had released a revised text just before the vote — billed as their "last, best and final" offer — that already included real concessions:

  • An ethics package Trump himself agreed to: officials would have to divest "large" crypto holdings or place them in blind trusts, with state attorneys general empowered to enforce it — a direct answer to the conflict-of-interest objection.
  • A narrowed Blockchain Regulatory Certainty Act (BRCA): its safe-harbor was cut back to Bank Secrecy Act / civil enforcement only, removing protection from criminal prosecution (including under Section 1960) — i.e., less of a shield for bad actors than the original draft.
  • A stablecoin "circuit breaker": if regulators saw large-scale deposits fleeing community banks into stablecoins, they could step in — with Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent holding final say.
  • The "Ag Amendment": new restrictions on vertical integration, related-party dealing, and conflicts of interest for digital commodity exchanges/brokers/dealers, plus explicit confirmation that state consumer-protection laws still apply, that developer protections don't exempt anyone from derivatives law, and that prediction markets are carved out untouched.

Even with all of that on the table, the bill still couldn't clear 50 "yes" votes — a sign the divide is deeper than any single provision, and that a rewritten text alone won't unlock 2026 passage.

Analysts (Bernstein, cited widely in the days after the vote) predicted exactly this: with Congress unable to pass a market-structure law in 2026, the SEC and CFTC would move on their own, "aggressively," using their existing exemptive and rulemaking authority — innovation exemptions for equity tokenization being one of the specific tools they flagged. This SEC order is that prediction playing out in real time.

Why this matters for planning

Without a statute, the "rules of the road" for tokenization are being written order-by-order, agency-by-agency, and can change with the next Commission, court challenge, or administration. That turns regulatory risk into business-model risk for every RWA platform, including MosaicAI: the ground you build on can move under a future SEC.

7What This Means for a Platform Like MosaicAI

MosaicAI tokenizes private real-world assets (primarily commercial real estate) under Regulation D 506(c), sells to verified accredited investors, and offers a peer-to-peer secondary market for those tokens. None of that is an "NMS stock." So it's worth separating the two questions the user asked: initial issuance, and secondary trading.

🏗️ Initial Issuance Not directly affected

  • MosaicAI's issuance flow (Reg D 506(c), accredited-investor verification via Parallel Markets, on-chain compliance via ONCHAINID) is untouched by this order — it applies to secondary trading venues for public stocks, not primary placements of private securities.
  • No new exemption exists (yet) that would let MosaicAI run a general-solicitation, AMM-priced initial sale of a private CRE deal the way an NMS stock could theoretically be listed on a TSV.
  • Indirect signal: the SEC is visibly comfortable with token-based compliance mechanics (wallet permissioning, smart-contract enforced rights) — this lowers the perceived risk of the compliance architecture MosaicAI already uses for issuance.

🔄 Secondary Trading Most relevant

  • MosaicAI's Genie Investor P2P secondary market today relies on manual matching between verified holders, bounded by Rule 144 holding periods and ongoing accreditation checks — not an AMM.
  • This order is the first concrete precedent of the SEC blessing an AMM-based, permissioned trading venue for tokenized securities instead of requiring full exchange/ATS registration. That is the exact structural problem private RWA platforms face if they ever want continuous, algorithmic secondary liquidity.
  • The conditions (issuer objection/block rights, no-synthetic-token rule, holder-rights parity, public auditable contracts, synchronized halts, disclosure) are effectively a preview checklist for what a future "private-securities TSV" exemption would likely require.
  • Directly actionable today: the "no synthetic tokens" and "issuer/sponsor objection right" standards map almost one-to-one onto what a sound private RWA platform should already guarantee — that each token is a genuine, redeemable claim on the underlying property, and that the deal sponsor formally consents to (and can restrict) how their asset's token trades. Formalizing that consent step now, before any regulator asks, mirrors exactly what Adam Aron is asking Robinhood to do voluntarily overseas.
The opportunity

The order's request for public comment is explicitly open-ended about "possible modifications and next steps." Bernstein's own read of the post-CLARITY landscape specifically calls out "innovation exemptions for equity tokenization" as a category regulators are expected to keep building out. That makes this a live, commentable proceeding MosaicAI (and the broader RWA industry) can engage with to advocate for an analogous private-market framework — rather than waiting years for a Reg D-specific version of this same idea.

9Frequently Asked Questions

The five questions that come up most when people first hear about this order — with a plain-English answer, and an honest flag for where the SEC hasn't actually said yet.

What equities and ETFs can be included — and what can't?
Mostly answered by the order

Included: only common stock already listed on a registered U.S. exchange ("NMS stock") qualifies, split into two tiers with hard caps: Tier 1 covers S&P 500 / Russell 1000 constituents — up to 75 symbols, each capped at 0.25% of that stock's average daily volume; Tier 2 covers other NMS stocks — up to 250 symbols, each capped at 2.5% of ADV.

Explicitly excluded: rights and warrants (carved out because the market-wide Limit Up-Limit Down plan the halt mechanism relies on excludes them too); and any "synthetic" product — a tokenized linked note or tokenized security-based swap that merely tracks a stock's price without being a real, redeemable claim on it. The SEC's own framing has been blunt that this exemption "is not for DeFi."

Genuinely unclear: whether ETFs are in scope. None of the SEC's public materials or the law-firm analyses we reviewed explicitly confirm or rule out ETFs, even though ETFs are technically also "NMS securities." Treat this as an open question until the full order text or a follow-up staff statement settles it — don't assume yes or no.

Categorically out of scope either way: private company shares — real estate, private equity, private credit, venture equity — which is exactly what MosaicAI issues.

What eligibility criteria will investors need? U.S. only, or any country?
Partly open question

What is specified: the TSV itself must be a U.S.-domiciled entity that screens for OFAC-sanctioned parties, and every participant — trader or liquidity provider — must be individually reviewed before getting access. Anonymous or fully open trading is explicitly banned.

What isn't specified: the order doesn't set a nationality restriction, a minimum net worth or accreditation threshold, or a specific KYC standard beyond "permissioned and sanctions-screened." Whether a retail investor in, say, the U.K. or India could participate — and under what tax-withholding or KYC regime — is left to each TSV's own onboarding design, and is squarely the kind of question the SEC's request for comment is inviting feedback on.

Practical expectation: TSVs will likely mirror existing broker-dealer/ATS-style KYC-AML onboarding (government ID, sanctions screening, tax-form collection) even though the order itself doesn't mandate a specific bar.

What will the tax reporting process be?
Answered — by the IRS, separately

This sits with the IRS rather than the SEC, but there's already a concrete mechanism: the IRS's new Form 1099-DA (digital-asset broker reporting) treats a tokenized security as a "dual-classification asset" — something that is both a security and a digital asset. Brokers handling these trades report on Form 1099-DA instead of the traditional Form 1099-B used for ordinary stock trades.

The IRS's average-cost-basis method (Section 1012(c)/(d)) remains available for a tokenized security where it would already be permitted for the underlying share, and wash-sale rules still apply and must be reported in the applicable 1099-DA fields — so shuffling between a tokenized share and its "real" counterpart to harvest a loss can still trigger wash-sale disallowance.

Net effect for an investor: broadly the same taxable events as owning the underlying share (dividends, capital gains/losses on sale) — just reported on a new form, with the broker or TSV responsible for issuing it.

Who will play the role of Transfer Agent, keeping track of tokenized stock ownership on TSV venues?
Open question — no answer in this order

The September 17 order itself does not create a new transfer-agent regime, does not require independent proof-of-reserve verification that a third-party tokenizer's tokens are actually backed 1:1 by real shares, and doesn't say who reconciles the on-chain ledger against the official shareholder registry.

It isn't a total void, though: two weeks earlier, on September 1, 2026, the SEC separately proposed its first overhaul of transfer-agent rules in more than 40 years, explicitly designed to accommodate blockchain-based recordkeeping of share ownership. That parallel proposal is the most likely place a formal answer emerges — probably existing SEC-registered transfer agents adapting to blockchain rails (a model firms like Securitize already operate through their own registered transfer-agent subsidiary).

In the meantime, TSVs are on the hook for trade-level recordkeeping — publishing prices, sizes, timestamps, pool addresses, end-of-day pool sizes, and daily volumes — but that's transaction transparency, not a legal share registry. This remains one of the most-cited gaps in outside legal analysis of the order.

Who will hold custody of these tokenized assets, and how?
Open question — no answer in this order

Also explicitly not mandated: there is no qualified-custodian requirement and no proof-of-reserve requirement written into the order. One real clue on intent: a liquidity-provider firm relying on the "dealer" exemption must trade solely for its own account and is expressly barred from holding or custodying customer assets — meaning customer-facing custody has to sit somewhere else, but the order doesn't say where.

Expect TSVs in practice to route custody through frameworks that already exist for regulated tokenization platforms — a mix of qualified custodians, SEC/state-chartered trust companies, and special-purpose broker-dealers for digital-asset securities — but there's no single, order-mandated answer yet. As one outside analysis of the order put it plainly: "governance, valuation, and custody questions do not disappear under this framework."

11Bottom Line & Suggested Action Items

Legally, nothing changes for MosaicAI's operations today. Reg D issuance rules and the existing P2P secondary-market constraints for private RWA tokens are unaffected by this order. Strategically, this is one of the most useful data points of the year: it's proof the SEC will let permissioned, smart-contract-based AMMs substitute for a registered exchange, and it arrives at the exact moment Congress ceded the field on crypto market structure by letting the CLARITY Act die.

  1. Submit a comment letter. The order is an open request for comment — MosaicAI (directly or via an industry group) can formally ask the SEC to study an analogous exemption for accredited-investor-only secondary trading of private tokenized securities.
  2. Build to the checklist now. Treat the order's conditions (auditable public smart contracts, synchronized halts, issuer/holder-rights parity, disclosure of venue-operator trading) as the likely template for any future private-market version, and get ahead of it in Genie Investor's architecture.
  3. Watch the SEC/CFTC rulemaking calendar, not Congress. With CLARITY dead for 2026, expect the next 12–24 months of relevant change to come from agency exemptive orders and no-action letters, not new legislation.
  4. Get ahead of custody and transfer-agent questions. These are the two biggest gaps in the current framework (Section 9) — a platform that can already answer them clearly for private RWA tokens has a real edge whenever regulators turn to that side of the market.
  5. Keep messaging precise. Avoid implying this exemption already covers MosaicAI's tokens — it doesn't. The honest, more powerful pitch is "the SEC is normalizing exactly the trading model we'd need for real secondary liquidity — we're building toward that."
