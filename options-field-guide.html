01 — Setup

What "directional bias" means

The skill's command is /trade options TICKER bullish|bearish|neutral. That last word is your directional bias — your honest opinion about where the stock is headed. It's the single input that decides which family of strategies even makes sense to look at.

You don't need a model to have a bias — it's just your answer to "do I think this stock goes up, down, or nowhere for a while?" Everything downstream (which strategy, which strikes, which side of the trade you're on) branches from that one answer.

Think of it like choosing a tool. A hammer and a wrench can both be "correct" — the only question is what you're actually trying to do. Bullish, bearish and neutral just point you at the right drawer before you start picking a specific strategy.
02 — Volatility

Implied Volatility (IV)

IV is the market's built-in guess of how much a stock will move over the next year — not which direction, just how far. It's expressed as an annualized percentage, and it's baked into every option's price.

Every option has a theoretical "fair" price based on time to expiration, the strike, interest rates, and one unknown: how wild the stock's future swings will be. Traders don't know that number — so instead, they work it backwards from what people are actually willing to pay for the option today. That backed-out number is implied volatility. High IV = expensive options because the market expects big swings. Low IV = cheap options because the market expects calm.

BAC's 30-day IV of ~23% means: if this rate held for a full year, the stock would be expected to move roughly ±23% (up or down) from today's price, about two-thirds of the time. Shrunk down to one month, that works out to the ±4.9% expected move you'll see in Section 05.

The umbrella-price analogy. If forecasters expect a storm this weekend, umbrellas at the corner store suddenly cost more — even though nobody actually knows if it will rain. IV is the "storm premium" priced into an option. It reflects expected turbulence, not a promise of it.
03 — Volatility, in context

IV Rank vs. IV Percentile

A raw IV number is meaningless on its own — 23% could be sky-high for a sleepy utility or dirt-cheap for a biotech. Rank and Percentile both answer "is today's IV high or low for this specific stock?" — but they answer it two different ways.

IV Rank asks: where does today's IV sit between the highest and lowest readings of the past 52 weeks?

iv_rank.pyformula
iv_rank = (current_iv - iv_52wk_low) / (iv_52wk_high - iv_52wk_low) * 100

IV Percentile asks a subtly different question: out of the last 252 trading days, what percentage had IV lower than today's?

iv_percentile.pyformula
iv_percentile = (days_with_lower_iv / total_trading_days) * 100
The weather analogy. Say this year's temperatures ranged from 30°F to 90°F, but the stock spent 300 of those days sitting near 32°F and only spiked to 90°F once, briefly. Today it's 60°F.

Rank only cares about the two extremes: (60−30)/(90−30) = 50% — "dead center," even though 60°F was actually a much warmer day than almost the entire year.
Percentile counts every day: if 90% of days were colder than 60°F, percentile reads 90% — "unusually warm," which is the more honest read of how that day actually felt.
Reading BAC's numbers

Rank (~50%) and Percentile (51%) happen to roughly agree here — which is itself informative. When both metrics land near the middle, it reinforces the report's own read: "no strong volatility mispricing to exploit either direction." When they disagree sharply, that's usually a sign one lone spike (often an old earnings event) is distorting the Rank number, and Percentile is the one to trust more.

04 — Volatility across time

Term Structure (Contango & Backwardation)

IV isn't one number — a stock has a different IV for every expiration date. Plot IV against days-to-expiration and you get the term structure: the volatility equivalent of a bond yield curve.

Contango (the "normal" shape): near-term IV is lower, and IV gently rises the further out you look. This makes intuitive sense — more time means more opportunity for something to happen, so the market prices in a bit more uncertainty.

Backwardation (the inverted shape): near-term IV is higher than longer-term IV. This shows up when a specific, dated event — earnings, an FDA ruling, a court decision — sits just before that near expiration. The option covering the event is bid up; the ones beyond it settle back down once that risk has passed.

Why it matters

Term structure tells you where the expensive volatility is parked. It's also the entire logic behind the Calendar Spread strategy in Section 07 — you sell the option sitting on the bump and buy the one that isn't.

05 — Sizing the move

Straddle & Expected Move

A straddle is buying (or selling) a call and a put at the same strike and expiration. It's a pure bet on how far the stock moves — direction doesn't matter.

A long straddle (buying both legs) makes money if the stock moves far enough in either direction to cover what you paid for both options combined. It loses the most if the stock does nothing and both options expire worthless at the strike. A short straddle is the mirror image — sold for that same combined premium — and profits if the stock stays pinned near the strike.

The reason a straddle gets its own section here: the price of an at-the-money straddle is the market's expected move. Add and subtract that dollar figure from the current price, and you get the range options are pricing in.

Worked example — expected move from a straddle price. BAC's report doesn't quote a raw straddle price directly, but its published ±4.9% monthly expected move implies one: about $3.04. Running it through the skill's own formula recovers exactly the range the report lists.

06 — Where the crowd is positioned

Max Pain

Max Pain is the single strike price at which the largest total dollar value of outstanding options — calls and puts combined — would expire worthless.

Because that's also the price where option sellers (often larger, better-hedged players like market makers) lose the least, some traders believe stocks have a mild gravitational pull toward max pain as expiration approaches — especially in quiet weeks with no news to override it. It's a crowd-behavior theory, not a law of physics — it's at its weakest right before something like earnings, when a real catalyst can easily overwhelm any "pull."

An honest limitation

BAC's own report couldn't pull a live max-pain figure — the data lives behind JS-rendered pages the research tools can't reach — so it estimated $60–62 from the put/call open-interest skew instead, and flagged it explicitly as an estimate rather than a sourced number. That's the right instinct: treat max pain as directional color, and confirm it on a live options-chain tool (a broker, or a site like a max-pain calculator) before it influences an actual trade.

Iron Condor

neutral · defined risk

Sell a put spread and a call spread at the same time, both out of the money. You collect two credits and profit if the stock stays between your two short strikes through expiration — the wings you bought cap the loss on either side.

Short Strangle

neutral · undefined risk

Sell an out-of-the-money call and an out-of-the-money put — same idea as an iron condor's middle, but with no protective wings bought. That missing insurance is the whole difference: losses are theoretically unlimited on the call side and very large (down to $0) on the put side.

Iron Butterfly

neutral · defined risk · narrow

Structurally identical to an iron condor, except both short strikes sit at the same price — usually right at the money — with wings still bought further out for protection. You're betting the stock pins almost exactly at one level, not merely "somewhere in a range."

Covered Call

bullish-to-neutral · income

Own 100 shares, then sell a call against them. You already carry the stock's full upside and downside; selling the call trades away upside above the strike in exchange for premium collected today. Downside is still the stock's downside, just cushioned slightly by that premium.

Max profit: (65-62.32)*100 + 35 = $303
If BAC falls: cushioned only by the $35 collected.
Effective cost basis: $61.97

Calendar Spread

neutral · term-structure play

Sell a near-term option and buy a longer-term option at the same strike. It profits as the near-term leg loses time value faster than the far leg — provided the stock stays near that strike. This is the direct payoff of a steep term structure: sell the expensive, bumped IV; buy the cheaper IV sitting behind it.

08 — Sample calculation

Estimating Probability of Profit

Once a credit spread is built, the natural next question is: how likely is it to actually work? There are two very different ways to estimate that — and they can disagree by a lot.

The quick, rough heuristic just compares the credit collected to the total width of the strikes:

credit_spread_pop.pyusing BAC's actual Bull Put Spread
credit_received = 0.44   # $61 put sold minus $59 put bought
width = 2.00              # distance between the two strikes

max_loss = width - credit_received
risk_reward = credit_received / max_loss
prob_of_profit_estimate = credit_received / width   # rough heuristic
Credit: $0.44  ·  Max Loss: $1.56  ·  Risk/Reward: 1 : 3.5
Rough heuristic POP: ≈ 22%
Why this doesn't match your report

BAC's own analysis lists this exact spread's probability of profit as ~69% — estimated from the short strike's delta instead. That's not a contradiction; it's two different questions. The credit/width ratio (22%) is a rough sanity check on how "cheap" the premium was relative to the max possible loss — it tends to run low for tight, low-credit spreads regardless of how likely they are to actually win. Delta-based POP (69%) approximates the market's own odds that the short option finishes in the money, which is much closer to a true statistical probability.

The rule of thumb: prefer delta when you have it, and only fall back to credit/width as a rough floor-level sanity check — never treat credit/width as the real probability.

09 — Loose ends

A Few More Terms

Three words that show up around volatility discussions but aren't strategy-specific.

Backwardation

When near-term implied volatility is priced higher than longer-term IV — the inverted, "something's coming soon" shape of the term structure curve.

↑ See Section 04

Contagion

When volatility or fear in one asset spreads to others that aren't directly related. A troubled regional bank can spike IV across every bank stock — BAC included — even though BAC's own fundamentals didn't change. Your IV can move for reasons that have nothing to do with your stock.

Baseline

The reference point everything else in a comparison is measured against. In the term-structure table, the front monthly IV is often the baseline — other expirations get described as a premium above it or a discount below it.
