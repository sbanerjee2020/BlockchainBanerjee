Part One · Explainer
Cryptography · plainly explained

Seeing Through the Lock

Why a 1994 algorithm — still waiting on hardware big enough to run it at full scale — is considered an existential threat to the math that secures your bank, your browser, and your crypto wallet.

Every time you check your bank balance, buy something online, or send Bitcoin, a padlock made of math snaps shut behind you. Nobody has ever picked that lock — not because people haven't tried, but because the amount of guessing required is absurd, bigger than the number of atoms worth counting. Shor's algorithm doesn't guess faster. It changes the kind of question being asked, and in doing so, makes the lock's difficulty evaporate.

01The lock itself: easy to close, brutal to pick

RSA — the encryption behind most of the "https" padlock icon you've clicked past a thousand times — is built on one asymmetry: multiplying two large prime numbers together is fast; splitting the result back into those two primes is not.

Locking it

2,053 × 3,299 = 6,776,847
Multiplication. Any calculator does this instantly, no matter how large the numbers get.

Picking it

6,776,847 = ? × ?
Given only the answer, finding the two primes means testing candidates one by one until something divides evenly.

For a 7-digit number like that, a laptop finds the answer in a blink. The trouble is how fast the difficulty grows. Real RSA keys don't use 7-digit numbers — they use numbers around 600 digits long. Every extra digit roughly doubles the classical search effort, so the difficulty doesn't creep upward, it explodes.

Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) — the scheme behind Bitcoin and Ethereum signatures, and most modern TLS connections — leans on a different but similarly lopsided problem, the discrete logarithm. Think of it like mixing two paint colors: combining them is trivial, but working out exactly which two colors and proportions produced the muddy result is (by design) a nightmare.

02What a quantum computer changes

A classical computer attacking either problem has only one move: try things, one at a time, and check each guess. That's the entire reason factoring is "hard" — there is no shortcut past exhaustive search.

A quantum computer running Shor's algorithm doesn't search harder. It exploits two quantum properties — superposition (a quantum bit can represent many candidate values at once) and interference (those overlapping possibilities can cancel out or reinforce each other, like waves in water). Shor showed that factoring a number secretly reduces to finding a repeating pattern — a period — hidden inside a sequence of numbers. Quantumly, you can set up all the candidates as overlapping waves simultaneously, and interference makes the correct period stand out as a peak.

It isn't a faster lockpick. It's a way of asking the lock a question that only has one possible answer left standing.

03A worked example small enough to do by hand

Let's factor N = 15 (we all know 15 = 3 × 5 — watch how the method finds it, then imagine the same steps on a 600-digit number where guessing is hopeless but the pattern-finding step is not).

Step 1 — pick a random number

Pick some number smaller than N that shares no common factor with it. We'll use a = 7.

7¹ mod 15= 7
7² mod 15= 4
7³ mod 15= 13
7⁴ mod 15= 1 ← repeats from here
The pattern's period is r = 4 — this is the number a quantum computer finds instantly via interference.
Step 2 — turn the period into factors

With an even period r = 4, compute 7^(r/2) = 7² = 49 ≡ 4 (mod 15), then two quick greatest-common-divisors:

gcd(4 − 1, 15)= gcd(3, 15) = 3
gcd(4 + 1, 15)= gcd(5, 15) = 5
15 = 3 × 5 — recovered without ever testing "is it divisible by 3?" directly.

The only step a classical machine cannot do efficiently is finding the period r in the first place. That's the entire quantum contribution: one hard step, swapped out for a quantum one. For discrete logarithms (the ECC/blockchain case), Shor's algorithm has an analogous trick — the same attack, not a separate one.

04So is my bank account in danger right now?

Not today. Running Shor's algorithm on a real 2048-bit RSA key needs on the order of thousands of stable, error-corrected logical qubits — today's best quantum computers don't have that. The real near-term risk already has a name — "harvest now, decrypt later" (HNDL). It doesn't need a working quantum computer today. It only needs one to exist eventually.

05Harvest now, decrypt later

The idea, as a story

Imagine a courier drives sealed, unbreakable strongboxes across town every day. A spy can't open a single one — so they stop trying, and instead copy every strongbox that goes by, storing the copies in a warehouse. No opening required — just collecting. When a master key finally exists, they open ten years of stored boxes all at once, retroactively.

The theft has, in all likelihood, already started. The only defense against a theft that already happened is to have encrypted that data with something quantum-resistant before it was stolen. There's no retroactive fix.

Actually at risk

Long shelf-life secrets: medical records, state secrets, source code, long-lived signing keys — anything meant to stay private for 10–30+ years.

Mostly fine

Short shelf-life secrets: a login session token, a live video call, a stock trade settled in seconds — stale long before decryption becomes possible.

06The fix: Post-Quantum Cryptography

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) is a confusing name for a simple idea: new lock designs, built to run on the computers we already have today, chosen because nobody — not even a future quantum computer — has found a fast way to pick them. Instead of factoring or discrete logs, PQC leans on problems like finding one short vector hidden among trillions of long ones in a high-dimensional lattice, or inverting a one-way hash function. No period, no repeating pattern — so Shor's trick has nothing to grab.

Compare that to RSA-2048's 256-byte public key. Every one of these locks is physically bigger — the real, un-glamorous price of quantum resistance.

How it actually ships: hybrid, not a swap

Almost nobody deploys PQC alone. Chrome, OpenSSH, Signal, and Cloudflare combine a classical algorithm and a post-quantum one for the same connection — you only get access if you break both. This exact pairing — X25519 + ML-KEM-768 — is what Chrome and OpenSSH already negotiate by default.

08Qubits, logical qubits, and CRQC

A qubit, versus a bit

A regular bit is a light switch: fully on or fully off. A qubit is more like a coin balanced on its edge, wobbling — leaning toward both at once until you measure it, when it snaps to a plain heads-or-tails. Quantum algorithms do their work while the coin is still spinning.

A real physical qubit is extremely delicate — a stray vibration can knock it flat before the calculation finishes (decoherence). The fix looks like RAID for hard drives: wire many noisy physical qubits together with an error-correcting code so the group behaves like one steady logical qubit. Today's chips carry hundreds to a couple thousand noisy physical qubits — and only recently (Google's "Willow" chip, Dec 2024) crossed the point where adding more physical qubits actually helps rather than hurts.

CRQC stands for Cryptographically Relevant Quantum Computer — not "a quantum computer exists," but one with enough logical qubits, speed, and reliability to actually break a real RSA-2048 or ECC-256 key. Today's machines are real and impressive, but none is a CRQC, and it isn't close.

Most public roadmaps place a real CRQC no earlier than the early-to-mid 2030s, with credible estimates decades further out — which is exactly why the migration to PQC is happening years ahead of that "when," not after it.

09Case study: how Ethereum is responding

Ethereum leans on three cryptographic systems a CRQC would break: ECDSA (signs ordinary accounts), BLS signatures (compress validator votes for consensus), and KZG commitments (make rollup data cheap to verify). Its growing use of STARK-based rollup proofs is hash-based by construction, and inherits quantum resistance essentially for free.

None of the replacements are live on mainnet as of 2026 — it's active research at pq.ethereum.org, targeting core infrastructure around 2029. The plan: validators move to a hash-based scheme (leanXMSS) paired with STARK-based aggregation; ordinary accounts get opt-in "signature agility" via account abstraction, no network-wide flag day; rollup data moves to STARK or lattice-based commitments.

One detail worth knowing: an Ethereum address is a hash of your public key, revealed only on your first outgoing transaction. An address that's never sent anything hasn't exposed its key yet. The instant it transacts, the key is public forever — and joins the pool a future CRQC could target.

The short version
  • RSA and ECC are secure because reversing a calculation is astronomically harder than doing it — for a classical computer.
  • Shor's algorithm reframes that reversal as a pattern-finding problem, and quantum interference finds the pattern directly.
  • Harvest now, decrypt later means today's encrypted data can be stolen and stored now, unlocked retroactively once the hardware exists.
  • Post-quantum cryptography — lattices, hashes, codes — runs on ordinary hardware today with no known quantum shortcut. NIST has finalized ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA, plus a backup (HQC).
  • For Python: pip install kyber-py dilithium-py gets a working demo in minutes; liboqs-python is the production step up.
  • A qubit is a fragile "coin still spinning"; a logical qubit is many wired together for reliability; a CRQC is the not-yet-built machine that could actually break real keys.
  • Ethereum is actively researching replacements for ECDSA, BLS, and KZG, targeting ~2029, nothing live yet.

Research briefing · quantum risk to cryptocurrency

The Quantum Freeze

Five sources look at the same threat and land in five different places — from "the threat is essentially fake" to a live proposal to permanently freeze Satoshi-era Bitcoin before a quantum computer gets the chance.

01Same threat, five conclusions

Every source agrees a big enough quantum computer could break the elliptic-curve cryptography securing Bitcoin and Ethereum. They split on when, how much it matters now, and whose job it is to fix it.

dismissive

Frames the threat as overhyped. Today's chips have "just over a hundred qubits" and are unstable prototypes; a real threat is "decades (or longer)" out.

"Bitcoin's cryptography can be swapped for quantum-resistant cryptography in an afternoon. The threat is essentially zero."— Kurt Wuckert Jr., CoinGeek
a16z cryptoDec 6, 2025
measured — but selective

Justin Thaler draws a sharp line: encryption is exposed to harvest-now-decrypt-later and deserves action today; signatures (what Bitcoin/Ethereum use) are not retroactively breakable the same way. Bitcoin's urgency, he argues, comes from its own slow governance — not an imminent quantum computer.

"The quantum threat to Bitcoin is real, but the timeline pressure comes from Bitcoin's own constraints, not from imminent quantum computers."— reported paraphrase of Justin Thaler, a16z crypto

02Is this actually "harvest now, decrypt later"?

HNDL in its classic form requires a secret: an attacker captures something encrypted today, can't read it yet, and reads it retroactively once a CRQC exists. A signature doesn't work that way — there's no hidden secret, and a confirmed transaction can't be un-confirmed. What a CRQC could do is derive a private key from an already-exposed public key and forge new transactions going forward.

The past transaction is safe forever. It's the still-unspent balance sitting at that same exposed address that isn't.

A TLS certificate rotates on a schedule — the old exposed key ages out. A Bitcoin address that has revealed its public key can't quietly rotate away; someone has to actively move the funds. For coins that are lost or abandoned, that will never happen — which is exactly why "measured, don't panic" gets harder to sustain for this one specific slice of Bitcoin.

03Key terms from these sources

CRQC — Cryptographically Relevant Quantum Computer
Needs an estimated hundreds of thousands to millions of physical qubits after error correction; today's largest machines have roughly 1,000–1,500.
HNDL — Harvest Now, Decrypt Later
NIST's IR 8547 names this as the reason encryption gets migration priority over authentication — a split that doesn't map cleanly onto Bitcoin's situation (see §2).
Mosca's inequality
NIST's decision rule: X (years data must stay safe) + Y (years to migrate) must not exceed Z (years to CRQC). Worked through for Bitcoin below.
Grover's algorithm
A much milder quantum algorithm — only a quadratic speedup against hash functions like SHA-256, cutting effective strength from 256 to 128 bits. Not a practical break.
BIP-360 / the freeze proposals
BIP-360 is the proposed quantum-resistant Bitcoin address format. At least two competing forced-migration proposals are circulating — Lopp's draft and a separate "QRAMP" proposal — itself a small sign of fragmented governance.

A secret with no natural expiration date fails the safety check under every estimate in this briefing — which is exactly why the freeze-proposal camp can claim NIST-consistent logic, not panic, as its justification.

How exposed is Bitcoin, really? 1.1M vs. 25%

Two oft-cited figures aren't in conflict — they're nested. ~19.9M BTC circulate; ~25% (~5M BTC) has ever exposed a public key (via old-format addresses, or simply by spending once); a much smaller ~1.1M BTC core is both exposed and dormant, with nobody left to move it. That smaller number is the one driving the freeze proposal — the other ~3.9M BTC can self-rescue any time, with zero protocol changes.

Circulating supply≈ 19.9M BTC
Exposed pubkey (any)≈ 4.9–5.0M BTC (~25%)
Dormant P2PK core≈ 1.1M BTC (can't self-rescue)
1.1M ≈ 5.5% of total supply, and ≈ 22% of the broader exposed-key pool.

05The Bitcoin freeze proposal, explained

Three phases, one clock

Phase A — 3 years after BIP-360 goes live: new deposits to legacy addresses are banned.

Phase B — 2 years after that: legacy signatures stop being valid at the consensus layer. Unmigrated coins become permanently unspendable.

Phase C (optional) — a zero-knowledge recovery path for anyone who can still prove they hold the original seed phrase.

Target: ~1.1 million BTC, including coins presumed to belong to Satoshi Nakamoto. No dissenting quote appears in this reporting — but the other four sources function as an implicit rebuttal even without addressing the proposal directly.

06What NIST's own guidance says

SystemNIST's logicDeadline
Encryption / key establishmentExposed to HNDL — highest priorityDeprecate 2030, disallow 2035
Authentication / signatures"Secure as long as keys are secure when used" — no HNDL exposureSame deadline, lower urgency

NIST's relaxed rule for signatures quietly assumes you can rotate to a fresh key before trouble arrives. That's true for a certificate — not for a dormant Bitcoin address whose key has been exposed for over a decade with nobody left to move it. Applied carefully to that specific case, NIST's own "relaxed" category stops sounding relaxed at all.

The short version
  • All five sources agree on the physics; they disagree on timeline, urgency, and who's responsible.
  • Bitcoin's exposure is "expose now, forge later," not classic HNDL — a public key sitting in the open becomes a spendable target the instant a CRQC exists.
  • That distinction is also NIST's own logic — but its relaxed timeline assumes keys can be rotated, which doesn't hold for dormant ledger addresses.
  • The 1.1M-BTC and 25%-of-supply figures nest, they don't compete — only the smaller number is stuck without help.
  • Applying NIST's own decision rule to a no-expiration-date asset argues for urgency under every estimate in this briefing.
